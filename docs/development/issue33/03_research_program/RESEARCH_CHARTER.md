# Ypsia Constitutional Internet Research Charter

**Status:** DRAFT — NOT YET APPROVED FOR PILOT EXECUTION  
**Version:** 0.1  
**Last updated:** 2026-08-08  
**Document role:** Proposed governing frame for Issue 33 research  
**Normative status:** Research-governance proposal; not constitutional text and not a repository of findings

Only Approved entries in DECISION_LOG.md are currently binding. Approval of this draft and limited pilot authority are still required before pilot evidence collection begins.

## 1. Mandate

This research programme investigates which structural characteristics of the contemporary sociotechnical Internet are constitutionally relevant to Ypsia.

Its purpose is to produce an evidence-grounded, explicitly fallible, and reviewable account of:

- structural conditions and power relations;
- mechanisms through which benefits and harms arise;
- affected persons, groups, institutions, communities, ecosystems, and future generations;
- boundary conditions, mitigations, and legitimate countervailing interests;
- the strength, limits, independence, and distribution of available evidence;
- relationships between candidate root problems, mechanisms, and consequences.

The programme diagnoses before it designs. It does not begin from a preferred technology, governance model, market structure, or constitutional remedy.

Existing Charter 3.0 material is a statement of founder vision and a source of hypotheses. It is not evidence. Its empirical diagnoses, concrete wording, and proposed solutions remain open to correction, revision, or rejection.

## 2. Primary research question

Which structural conditions and mechanisms across the contemporary sociotechnical Internet materially affect human autonomy, equal moral worth and rights, collective knowledge, democratic and legal agency, resilience, and ecological or intergenerational interests; under what conditions do they produce benefits or harms; and which sufficiently evidenced patterns are constitutionally relevant to Ypsia?

### 2.1 Supporting questions

For each candidate phenomenon:

1. What is the exact claim?
2. At which layer or cross-layer interaction does it arise?
3. Is it a possible root problem, mechanism, consequence, affected interest, example, tension, mitigation, or solution constraint?
4. Who or what is affected, including people who do not directly use the system?
5. What mechanism connects the structural condition to the claimed effect?
6. Under which conditions does the effect occur, diminish, reverse, or fail to appear?
7. What benefits, legitimate purposes, mitigations, and alternative explanations exist?
8. How strong, direct, independent, and applicable is the evidence?
9. What is the strongest credible counterevidence?
10. Why, if at all, is the phenomenon within Ypsia's constitutional reach?

## 3. Research object: the sociotechnical Internet

Subject to ratification of DEC-005, the Internet is provisionally studied as an interacting system of six layers:

1. **Physical and ecological infrastructure**  
   Energy, water, minerals, hardware, chips, cables, data centres, logistics, labour, land use, waste, and ecological effects.

2. **Protocols and technical architecture**  
   Standards, addressing, identity, interoperability, security, decentralisation, dependencies, resilience, failure modes, and control points.

3. **Applications, platforms, and interfaces**  
   Services, platforms, recommender systems, user interfaces, accessibility, choice architecture, content mediation, and digital public space.

4. **Data, algorithmic, and AI systems**  
   Data collection, aggregation, inference, models, automated decisions, training, provenance, synthetic content, and machine-mediated knowledge.

5. **Ownership, market, and revenue models**  
   Property, contractual power, concentration, lock-in, extraction, labour relations, incentives, financing, and distribution of value.

6. **Law, governance, and social institutions**  
   Legislation, public administration, courts, regulators, democratic institutions, education, media, communities, and other institutions that shape or depend on digital systems.

Cross-layer interactions are primary research objects. A phenomenon must not be reduced to its most visible technical layer when its causes or consequences lie elsewhere.

## 4. Scope boundaries

The programme includes:

- public and private digital power;
- direct and indirect effects on users and non-users;
- individual, relational, collective, institutional, ecological, and intergenerational interests;
- benefits and harms structurally connected to one or more of the six layers;
- sector-specific cases when they illuminate a potentially generalisable mechanism;
- global evidence, with geography, jurisdiction, population, sector, and period always recorded.

The programme does not treat as a root Internet problem:

- a general social problem without a distinct digitally mediated structural mechanism;
- an isolated incident without broader evidentiary relevance;
- a proposed solution's implementation difficulty, unless retained explicitly as a later solution constraint;
- a product requirement, constitutional remedy, or Charter formulation;
- a prediction presented as fact without an explicit uncertainty classification.

The health application is the historical origin and may become a proving ground. It does not limit the research object to health or to one application.

## 5. Normative starting point and epistemic openness

The founder has confirmed the following moral ground:

- every person has equal moral worth, independent of background;
- technology is a means that must serve life rather than become an end in itself;
- the benefits of foundational technology must not depend on privilege or exclusion;
- technological development should contribute to a sustainable balance between people, their environments, and Earth;
- the founder's perception, vision, and proposed solutions may grow or change in response to evidence.

These commitments guide which interests and constitutional questions deserve attention. They do not establish that any empirical diagnosis is true, that a particular causal mechanism exists, or that Ypsia's current architecture is an effective remedy.

The research therefore separates:

1. **Normative commitments:** moral choices that science can inform but not prove.
2. **Empirical diagnoses:** falsifiable claims about existence, distribution, magnitude, causality, and conditions.
3. **Solution hypotheses:** falsifiable claims that an intervention or architecture improves the diagnosed condition.
4. **Constitutional reasoning:** the later, explicit bridge between evidence, values, competing interests, and binding principles.

## 6. Equality standard

The programme adopts the following approved baseline:

> Every person possesses unconditional equal moral worth and the same fundamental digital rights, protection, and legal standing. Differences in implementation are permitted only when demonstrably necessary to make those same rights effectively accessible. They must never reduce the content, protection threshold, or contestability of those rights.

Any differentiated treatment, accommodation, implementation, or safeguard must satisfy all of the following:

1. it pursues the same right and substantive goal;
2. the relevant difference is demonstrated and not inferred from stereotype;
3. the differentiation is necessary;
4. no less differentiating effective alternative is available;
5. the universal minimum content and protection threshold remain intact;
6. it creates no arbitrary privilege, dependency, or concentration of power;
7. it is transparent, contestable, and periodically reviewable;
8. the burden of proof rests on the proponent of the differentiation.

Needs, cultures, and circumstances may inform implementation only through this test. They do not compete with or override equality.

Equality is a cross-cutting evaluative safeguard. It does not predetermine that every observed disparity is a root problem, nor does it permit empirical differences to be ignored.

## 7. Neutral diagnosis-first principle

Research questions and candidate claims must be formulated so that credible evidence can narrow, qualify, or refute them.

Neutrality requires:

- separating empirical existence, prevalence, magnitude, and causality from normative relevance;
- recording benefits, legitimate purposes, successful mitigations, and null findings;
- actively examining alternative explanations and contrary evidence;
- avoiding assumptions that centralisation, decentralisation, public control, private control, openness, automation, or human decision-making are inherently superior;
- distinguishing unavoidable properties from contingent design, governance, or incentive choices;
- stating what the evidence does not establish;
- preserving corrected and rejected hypotheses in the audit trail.

Neutrality does not require neutrality about evidence quality, logical validity, equal moral worth, or adopted constitutional safeguards.

A proposed remedy is never evidence that a diagnosis is correct. The absence of a remedy is never evidence that a diagnosis is false.

## 8. Epistemic commitments

The programme commits to:

- claim-level traceability;
- transparent provenance;
- systematic discovery followed by targeted synthesis;
- source independence rather than source counting;
- design-appropriate quality appraisal;
- active counterevidence searching;
- explicit treatment of uncertainty and heterogeneity;
- separation of empirical evidence and normative recognition;
- historical preservation of corrections and rejected hypotheses;
- reproducible search, screening, and decision records;
- correction without erasure.

A minimum of two independent, verifiable, and claim-relevant sources is required before a candidate can be proposed as an independent problem or problem family. This is an admissibility threshold, not proof by counting.

The intended standard is an academically defensible and auditable research process. The work must not be described as peer-reviewed research unless it has actually undergone an applicable independent peer-review process.

## 9. Decision boundary

The research programme may produce:

- candidate and claim registers;
- evidence maps;
- workstream syntheses;
- a provisional causal and problem topology;
- separate empirical evidence, formulation, and normative-recognition assessments;
- red-team findings;
- recommendations to retain, merge, reclassify, hold, correct, or reject candidates.

It does not itself establish:

- permanent constitutional problem IDs;
- rights, prohibitions, mandates, principles, or architectural requirements;
- final Charter language;
- product or implementation requirements.

Human approval determines normative scope and constitutional relevance. Human approval cannot upgrade the empirical strength of evidence. Evidence classifications must remain attributable to the recorded evidence and review process.

## 10. Review and challenge

Every sufficiently developed workstream synthesis and every proposed topology must undergo structured adversarial review.

Review must test:

- claim precision;
- source quality and independence;
- causal reasoning;
- missing stakeholders and layers;
- equality implications;
- contrary evidence and alternative explanations;
- mitigation and residual harm;
- overlap and double counting;
- constitutional relevance.

Where possible, the formal reviewer should not be the primary synthesist. If this is impossible, review must occur in a separate cold-context pass with the original synthesis treated as an object to be challenged.

## 12. Change control

Before first approval, edits to this draft remain visible through Git and are summarised in STATUS.md. Once an approved pilot or production version is frozen, a change requires:

- a stated reason;
- a version increment;
- an impact assessment for ongoing and completed research;
- an approved decision-log entry;
- re-review of affected conclusions where necessary.

Progress updates, new sources, and candidate findings do not modify an approved Research Charter.
